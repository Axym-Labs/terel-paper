\publicationabstract{%
Slow Feature Analysis (SFA) learns persistent factors by making adjacent
representations similar while preserving their variance and decorrelating
their coordinates.  Can these three requirements train a deep nonlinear
encoder without carrying an error through depth or time?  We derive a
regularized target from a soft SFA objective and map its residual to the
preactivation of each neuron.  Without lateral coupling, the outer product of
this neuron state and presynaptic activity is the exact feedforward gradient.
Learned inhibition then settles the state used for the feedforward update, and
pairs of the same states drive an anti-Hebbian lateral rule.

The resulting method, Temporal Regularized Learning--Samplewise (\TeReLS{}),
processes one observation at a time, retains fixed detached state, and sends no
error between layers.  On label-ordered MNIST, it reaches
$\FinalTeReLSAccuracy\pm\FinalTeReLSSD$\% test accuracy after two data
presentations.  On a train-derived validation split, residual-state inhibition
improves a matched samplewise reference by $3.01$ percentage points while
preserving effective rank.  A minibatch relaxation, \TeReLBatched{}, reaches
$97.30\pm0.07$\%, compared with $98.34\pm0.08$\% for presentation-matched
backpropagation and $96.98\pm0.10$\% for Local SupCon.  Objective ablations and
a direct-covariance control identify the roles of the three regularizers and
the lagged decorrelation signal.  A chronological PAMAP2 comparison is
inconclusive.  The evidence establishes local learning under a controlled
temporal relation; it does not show that arbitrary natural adjacency is
informative.}

\section{Introduction}

The observations in a temporal stream may change faster than the causes that
produce them.  A useful representation should then move slowly.  Slowness
alone, however, has an immediate failure: every observation can map to the same
point.  SFA removes this solution by requiring nonzero variance and
decorrelated coordinates~\cite{wiskott2002}.  These requirements define the
learning problem: slow, noncollapsed, and nonredundant representations.

Our question is whether they also define a learning rule that is local in
space and time.  Spatial locality means that a synapse receives quantities
available at its endpoints, possibly after same-layer neural dynamics, but no
error from a later layer.  Temporal locality means that the update reads a
fixed detached state rather than an activation graph extending into the past.
These are restrictions on credit assignment, not claims of neuron independence
or biological realism.

\TeReLS{} realizes both restrictions by working backward from the soft-SFA
gradient.  The three objective terms first define a detached regularized
target.  Each neuron then tracks its preactivation residual to this target.
For a linear synapse, the gradient factors into the neuron's residual state and
the presynaptic activity.  We use pairs of the same residual states to learn a
dense inhibitory operator.  Both factors of the feedforward rule are available
at the synapse, giving it a Hebbian two-factor form; the signed
lateral change is anti-Hebbian.  \Cref{sec:method} states both signs explicitly.

Inhibitory settling of the residual state is the central distinction between
\TeReLS{} and the exact detached soft-SFA gradient.  In a matched batch-size-one
experiment, it recovers $3.01$ accuracy points without sacrificing effective
rank.  \TeReLBatched{} provides a less restrictive performance reference.  It
retains adjacent activations in a short computation graph and uses minibatch
normalization; it is not the definition of the method.

The source of temporal order bounds what these experiments establish.  We
construct the primary MNIST stream from label-homogeneous chunks.  The labels
determine adjacency but do not enter the \TeReLS{} objective.  This setting
isolates a task-aligned temporal relation; it is not unlabeled
self-supervision.  A secondary experiment uses chronological wearable-sensor
measurements without activity classes in the encoder loss.  Its result is
inconclusive.

The paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We derive a regularized target and a preactivation residual state whose
  outer product with presynaptic activity is the exact feedforward gradient of
  a detached surrogate.
  \item We learn inhibitory lateral connections from the same neuron states and
  state the resulting locality boundary and $O(D^2)$ cost exactly.
  \item We evaluate the samplewise rule, a batched relaxation, Local SupCon,
  direct covariance, and the objective mechanisms under one controlled MNIST
  construction, with selection separated from final evaluation.
  \item We distinguish the controlled result from the unresolved question of
  learning from natural temporal order.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Temporal Regularized Learning}\label{sec:method}

We derive the method in dependency order.  A soft-SFA objective first defines
a target.  The target defines a neuron state.  That state then determines the
feedforward and lateral updates.

\subsection{A soft-SFA target}

At one layer, let
\begin{equation}
  a_t=W x_t+c,\qquad z_t=\phi(a_t),\qquad z_t\in\mathbb R^D,
\end{equation}
where $x_t$ is detached from the preceding learned layer.  The layer stores a
detached predecessor $p_t$, running mean $m$, running variance $v$, and an
off-diagonal second-moment operator $A$.  A boundary indicator sets $I_t=0$
when $p_t$ belongs to another sequence and $I_t=1$ otherwise.  For a local
batch of size $B$, let $N_I=\sum_t I_t$.  The soft objective is
\begin{align}
L_S &= \frac{1}{N_ID}\sum_t I_t\|z_t-p_t\|_2^2,\label{eq:similarity}\\
L_V &= -\frac{1}{BD}\sum_{t,j}[\gamma-v_j]_+(z_{t,j}-m_j)^2,
\label{eq:variance}\\
L_C &= \frac{1}{BD}\sum_t(z_t-m)^\top A\stopgrad(z_t-m),
\label{eq:covariance}\\
L &= \lambda_SL_S+\lambda_VL_V+\lambda_CL_C.\label{eq:terel}
\end{align}
If $N_I=0$, we set $L_S=0$.  The first term makes adjacent representations
similar.  The second expands coordinate $j$ while its stored variance is below
$\gamma$; its negative sign is essential.  The third uses a stored operator to
oppose correlated representation coordinates.  All stored quantities are
constants during differentiation.

These three forces can be written as one target.  Define $g=[\gamma-v]_+$ and
$\omega_t=BI_t/N_I$ when $N_I>0$ (zero otherwise).  From detached values at the
current iterate, form
\begin{align}
r_t={}&\omega_t(z_t-p_t)
-\frac{\lambda_V}{\lambda_S}g\odot(z_t-m)
+\frac{\lambda_C}{2\lambda_S}A(z_t-m),\label{eq:activation-residual}\\
\hat z_t={}&\stopgrad(z_t-r_t).\label{eq:regularized-target}
\end{align}
The vector $\hat z_t$ is not an externally supplied label.  It is the local
activation that would cancel the instantaneous combination of temporal,
variance, and decorrelation forces.

\begin{proposition}[Target equivalence]\label{prop:target}
At the iterate where \cref{eq:regularized-target} is constructed, the detached
target loss
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde L_z=\frac{\lambda_S}{BD}
  \sum_t\|z_t-\hat z_t\|_2^2
  \label{eq:target-loss}
\end{equation}
has the same derivative with respect to $z_t$ as \cref{eq:terel}.
\end{proposition}
The result follows by substituting $z_t-\hat z_t=r_t$ into
$\partial\widetilde L_z/\partial z_t=2\lambda_Sr_t/(BD)$.  The target compresses
the three local gradients without changing them.

\subsection{From target residual to neuron state}\label{sec:neuron-state}

The activation residual $r_t=z_t-\hat z_t$ is not yet a neuron state at the
input to a feedforward synapse.  We map it through the activation Jacobian,
\begin{equation}
  b_t=J_{\phi,t}^{\top}r_t,
  \qquad J_{\phi,t}=\left.\frac{\partial\phi(a)}{\partial a}\right|_{a_t}.
  \label{eq:base-neuron-state}
\end{equation}
For the elementwise LeakyReLU used here,
$b_t=r_t\odot\phi'(a_t)$.  Here $b_{t,j}$ is the preactivation residual tracked
by neuron $j$, not an error delivered from another layer.

We next let same-layer inhibition act on this state.  Let $M\succeq0$ denote
the learned second moment of the neuron states and initialize
$s_t^{(0)}=b_t$.  A short local settling process applies
\begin{equation}
s_t^{(q+1)}=s_t^{(q)}+\eta
\left(b_t-s_t^{(q)}-\kappa M s_t^{(q)}\right),
\quad q=0,\ldots,Q-1.
\label{eq:residual-dynamics}
\end{equation}
The final neuron state is $s_t=s_t^{(Q)}$.  At equilibrium,
$s_t=(I+\kappa M)^{-1}b_t$; the implementation uses four Euler steps.  Setting
$\kappa=0$ recovers $s_t=b_t$ and hence the exact soft-objective gradient.
Nonzero inhibition is a controlled modification of that gradient, not an
algebraic identity.

Now define the detached preactivation target
$\hat a_t=\stopgrad(a_t-s_t)$.  Its mean-squared error gives
\begin{equation}
\widetilde L_a=\frac{\lambda_S}{BD}\sum_t\|a_t-\hat a_t\|_2^2,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\widetilde L_a}{\partial W}
=\frac{2\lambda_S}{BD}\sum_t s_t x_t^\top,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\widetilde L_a}{\partial c}
=\frac{2\lambda_S}{BD}\sum_t s_t.
\label{eq:feedforward-gradient}
\end{equation}
This is the intended two-factor Hebbian form at the level of the gradient:
presynaptic activity times the state of the postsynaptic neuron.  Plain
gradient descent would use
$\Delta W=-\alpha(2\lambda_S/BD)\sum_t s_tx_t^\top$; the experiments pass the
same factorized gradient to AdamW.  We do not absorb the descent sign into the
word ``Hebbian.''

\subsection{Anti-Hebbian learning on the same states}

After the parameter update, the same detached neuron states update $M$:
\begin{equation}
M^+ = \rho_M M +(1-\rho_M)\frac{1}{BD}\sum_t s_ts_t^\top.
\label{eq:residual-lateral-update}
\end{equation}
The positive-semidefinite matrix $M$ stores residual-state second moments and
appears with the negative sign $-\kappa Ms$ in
\cref{eq:residual-dynamics}.  Equivalently, the signed lateral weight is
$L^{\rm lat}=-\kappa M$, whose update contains
$-\kappa(1-\rho_M)s_{t,j}s_{t,k}$.  Positive coactivity makes
$L^{\rm lat}_{jk}$ more negative, which is the anti-Hebbian convention used
here.  Both endpoints expose neuron states: the lateral update uses
$s_{t,j}s_{t,k}$, not a residual paired with an unrelated activation.  The
diagonal of $M$ is nonnegative and acts as learned self-inhibition; the
off-diagonal entries connect distinct neurons and need not be elementwise
nonnegative.

The representation operator $A$ and the inhibitory operator $M$ serve
different purposes.  The former constructs the decorrelation component of
$\hat z_t$; the latter acts on the resulting neuron states.  We retain $A$
because replacing its representation statistic by residual-state covariance
would change the objective rather than merely realize its gradient.

The operator $A$ is a lagged proxy for the current representation covariance.
Let
\begin{equation}
C_k=\offdiag\left[\frac1B\sum_t(z_{k,t}-m_{k-1})
(z_{k,t}-m_{k-1})^\top\right].
\end{equation}
The direct penalty $\|C_k\|_F^2/D$ has activation gradient
$4C_k(z_{k,t}-m_{k-1})/(BD)$.  Replacing $C_k$ by stored $A_{k-1}$ gives the
direction in \cref{eq:activation-residual}, up to a positive scale.  This is a
lagged gradient proxy, not an equality between scalar objectives.  The direct
control in \cref{sec:results} tests whether the approximation matters.

\subsection{Execution order and locality}\label{sec:gradients}

The layer reads all state before it writes any state.  After computing
$z_t$, $\hat z_t$, and $s_t$, it updates $W$ using
\cref{eq:feedforward-gradient}.  It then stores the current detached activation
and updates
\begin{align}
m^+ &= \rho_m m+(1-\rho_m)\bar z,\label{eq:mean-update}\\
v^+ &= \rho_m v+(1-\rho_m)(z_t-m)^2,\label{eq:var-update}\\
A^+ &= \rho_A A+(1-\rho_A)\offdiag[(z_t-m)(z_t-m)^\top],
\label{eq:lat-update}
\end{align}
along with \cref{eq:residual-lateral-update}.  A boundary flag invalidates the
stored predecessor.  No activation graph survives the step.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{One samplewise \TeReLS{} update.}
\label{alg:terel}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require observation $x_t$, boundary $I_t$, parameters $W,c$, detached state
  $p,m,v,A,M$
\State $a_t\gets W\stopgrad(x_t)+c$; $z_t\gets\phi(a_t)$
\State construct $\hat z_t$ by \cref{eq:activation-residual,eq:regularized-target}
\State $b_t\gets J_{\phi,t}^{\top}(z_t-\hat z_t)$
\State settle $s_t$ by \cref{eq:residual-dynamics}
\State update $W,c$ from the detached target
  $\hat a_t\gets\stopgrad(a_t-s_t)$
\State update $p,m,v,A,M$ from detached $z_t,s_t$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\Cref{alg:terel} is applied independently at each learned layer in the same
forward pass.  A layer receives the detached activation of its predecessor and
owns its target, neuron state, and running matrices.

At a feedforward synapse $W_{ji}$, \cref{eq:feedforward-gradient} uses only the
presynaptic activity $x_i$ and postsynaptic state $s_j$.  Computing $s_j$ may
use signals delivered by lateral connections $M_{jk}$.  Each such connection
updates from the states at its two endpoints.  No error crosses a learned
layer boundary, so the rule is neuron-local in space but not
neuron-independent.  The predecessor, moments, and lateral matrices are
detached and fixed in size; the rule is also local in time.

\subsection{The batched relaxation}

\TeReLBatched{} is a performance reference that relaxes temporal locality.  It
optimizes \cref{eq:terel} directly on class-homogeneous chunks, keeps
$p_t=z_{t-1}$ live within a chunk, and uses BatchNorm.  The temporal derivative
then includes a future term,
\begin{equation}
\frac{2\lambda_S}{N_ID}
\left[I_t(z_t-z_{t-1})-I_{t+1}(z_{t+1}-z_t)\right].
\label{eq:batch-gradient}
\end{equation}
This derivative requires a short temporal graph.  Training-mode BatchNorm also
couples examples within each coordinate.  BatchNorm is an optimization choice
in this relaxation, not a fourth part of the method and not part of the
\TeReLS{} locality claim.

\subsection{State and compute cost}\label{sec:cost}

For a width-$D$ layer, \TeReLS{} stores $m,v,p$, the preceding centered
activation, a validity flag, and two $D\times D$ matrices $A,M$: in total,
$2D^2+4D+1$ scalars.  The state is independent of stream length but quadratic
in width.  Each of the $Q$ settling steps also applies a dense matrix--vector
product.  The state is discarded at inference.  Parameters, optimizer moments,
temporary activations, and the two lateral matrices are reported separately;
bounded history alone implies neither low memory nor efficient hardware.

\section{Relation to prior work}\label{sec:related}

Two lines of work meet here.  SFA supplies the objective; local representation
learning supplies the credit-assignment question.  \TeReLS{} combines them
through a target--state construction with an explicit execution boundary.

\paragraph{SFA and incremental SFA.}
Temporal slowness, variance preservation, and decorrelation define the SFA
objective~\cite{wiskott2002}.  Linear batch SFA whitens the observations and
extracts minor components of their temporal differences.  IncSFA replaces
these batch operations with incremental PCA and minor-component updates that
never form a covariance matrix.  IncSFA also handles episodic boundaries and
has Hebbian and anti-Hebbian update forms~\cite{kompella2012}.  It is the
relevant online precedent rather than an alternative implementation of
batch SFA.  Our
empirical baseline is a Python 3 port of the published linear algorithm,
validated against batch SFA on a synthetic stream.  \TeReLS{} instead applies
a soft objective to each learned nonlinear layer and stores dense lateral
operators.

PowerSFA trains deep SFA models end to end by differentiating through an
approximate whitening operation~\cite{schuler2018}.  Deep gradient-based SFA
predates this work.  \TeReLS{} instead blocks gradients between
learned layers and across temporal history.  Temporal
coherence has also been used directly in deep encoders.  Slow and Steady
Feature Analysis, for example, regularizes first- and higher-order changes
across video tuples~\cite{jayaraman2016}.  We restrict attention to first-order
adjacency to study a neuron-local realization with bounded temporal state.

\paragraph{Bio-SFA and trace learning.}
Bio-SFA derives an online neural network for SFA with local synaptic
updates~\cite{lipshutz2020}.  Neither online SFA nor local SFA is new here.
\TeReLS{} differs in its deep soft-penalty parameterization, residual neuron
state, and explicit stop-gradient implementation for nonlinear layers.  F\"oldi\'ak-style
trace learning also exploits temporal persistence~\cite{foldiak1991}.  Its
positive activation trace is algebraically distinct from the signed temporal
difference in \cref{eq:activation-residual}.

\paragraph{Gradient-isolated predictive and contrastive learning.}
Greedy InfoMax trains a stack of gradient-isolated modules with local InfoNCE
objectives that predict along the natural order of images, audio, and
video~\cite{lowe2019}.  It is the closest precedent for combining temporal
structure with greedy depth-local representation learning.  CLAPP makes
contrastive prediction causal and local across both depth and time.  Its update
uses pre- and postsynaptic activity, predictive dendritic input, and broadcast
modulation, but still requires positive and negative examples~\cite{illing2021}.
LoCo trains overlapping blocks with paired-view contrastive objectives.  The
loss of the next block can update parameters in the overlap, so LoCo relaxes
strict gradient isolation~\cite{xiong2020}.

Local temporal prediction and gradient-isolated deep learning are prior art.
\TeReLS{} studies a different objective: a
noncontrastive soft-SFA loss with explicit variance and redundancy control.
It uses neither a negative set nor a predictive decoder, but it requires dense,
lagged, same-layer operators.  Its main MNIST experiment also uses
labels to construct temporal order, unlike the principal unlabeled settings of
these predictive methods.

\paragraph{Regularized and blockwise self-supervision.}
VICReg and Barlow Twins prevent collapse without negative examples by
penalizing variance and covariance, or cross-correlation,
respectively~\cite{bardes2022,zbontar2021}.  Blockwise self-supervised learning
has shown that VICReg, Barlow Twins, and contrastive losses can train network
blocks without end-to-end backpropagation~\cite{siddiqui2024}.  \TeReLS{}
differs in its temporal relation, detached running moments, target--state
construction, and same-layer covariance proxy.  These choices permit
one-observation-at-a-time execution.

The execution details further separate the methods.  Siddiqui et al. find that
simultaneous block training is materially stronger than sequential freezing,
showing that forward co-adaptation remains useful after gradients
are stopped at block boundaries~\cite{siddiqui2024}.  \TeReLBatched{} instead
uses greedy layerwise training.  Datta et al. apply VICReg losses to individual
layers using original--augmented image pairs and current-batch variance and
covariance terms~\cite{datta2025}.  \TeReLS{} uses a temporal relation, a gated
detached variance state, and two lagged lateral statistics.

The positive relation itself matters.  Spectral analyses show that it can
determine downstream geometry when it aligns with task labels
~\cite{balestriero2022}.  Our class-chunk MNIST experiment should be
interpreted as constructed pair supervision and compared with a label-aware
local baseline.  It does not show that arbitrary natural adjacency reveals
class structure.

\section{Experimental protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

The experiments ask four questions.  Does samplewise \TeReLS{} learn a useful,
noncollapsed representation?  Does the residual-state mechanism improve the
same samplewise rule without inhibition?  How does the batched relaxation
compare with local and end-to-end baselines?  Does chronological order help on
a natural sensor stream?

\subsection{Data splits and roles}

\paragraph{MNIST controlled temporal supervision.}
We stratify the 60,000-example MNIST training split into 50,000 examples for
fitting and 10,000 for validation.  Final accuracy is measured on the official
held-out split.  At each encoder epoch, training examples are randomly arranged
into same-class chunks of length 16, with a boundary at every chunk start.
Labels determine this order but never enter a \TeReL{} objective.  The protocol
isolates a task-aligned temporal signal and is not unlabeled self-supervision.

\paragraph{PAMAP2 secondary natural-order stress test.}
PAMAP2 provides 100-Hz wearable-sensor measurements and activity annotations
for nine subjects~\cite{reiss2012pamap2}.  We retain its 12 protocol
activities, remove transition label 0 as directed by the dataset documentation,
and downsample with stride 10.  Missing sensor values are imputed with means
from the training subjects, and all features are standardized with statistics
from those subjects.

Subjects 1--6 fit the encoder and probe, subject 7 supplies validation data,
and subject 8 supplies the held-out evaluation.  Subject 9 is excluded because
its protocol recording contains only rope jumping and cannot support a
12-class evaluation.  Temporal boundaries occur at subject starts, gaps left
by transition rows, and timestamp discontinuities.  Encoder batches contain
only features and boundaries.  This experiment tests the temporal intuition;
it does not replace the controlled MNIST comparison.

\subsection{Models, baselines, and readouts}

All MNIST encoders have dimensions $784\rightarrow512\rightarrow256$ and use
LeakyReLU with negative slope 0.01.  \TeReLS{} trains both layers jointly, one
sample at a time, for two data presentations.  It uses identity normalization,
AdamW with learning rate $6\times10^{-6}$ and zero weight decay,
$(\lambda_S,\lambda_V,\lambda_C)=(1,2.5,1)$, $\gamma=1$, and momenta
$\rho_m=\rho_A=\rho_M=0.99$.  Residual inhibition uses $\kappa=1000$, four
settling steps of size $0.1$, and the feature-normalized full second moment in
\cref{eq:residual-lateral-update}.  Every sample causes one optimizer update.

The matched samplewise reference changes only the residual-state inhibition:
it applies the exact detached soft-objective gradient with $M=0$.  This comparison
isolates the mechanism introduced in \cref{sec:neuron-state}; architecture,
order, normalization, optimizer, state timescales, and data presentations are
identical.

\TeReLBatched{} uses the same hidden dimensions and activation, affine
BatchNorm, live temporal pairs within each chunk, and greedy training for 30
epochs per layer, for 60 data presentations.  AdamW uses
learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and batch size 256.
Its loss coefficients are $(1,2.5,1)$ and its population momentum is 0.9.
The next minibatch uses the representation operator written by the preceding
one.  This configuration is an optimization-rich reference, not a spatially
or temporally matched control for \TeReLS{}.

The backpropagation baseline matches the \TeReLBatched{} architecture,
normalization, optimizer, batch size, and 60 data presentations, and trains end
to end with cross entropy.  Two random encoders separate learning from
normalization.  One omits normalization; the other keeps all hidden and affine
BatchNorm parameters at initialization and updates only BatchNorm running
statistics in one fitting-split pass.  This calibration is data-dependent
normalization, not representation learning.

Local SupCon is a label-aware local comparator for the constructed stream.
Each layer minimizes supervised contrastive loss over all same-label positives
in its minibatch, and activations are detached between layers.  It matches the
\TeReLBatched{} architecture, BatchNorm, batch size, 60 data presentations,
all-layer probe, and evaluation runs.  Its learning rate and temperature are
selected from a fixed $3\times2$ validation grid.

Every primary representation is evaluated by the same linear classifier on
the concatenation of both hidden layers.  A second probe reads only the final
layer of the identical \TeReLBatched{} encoder.  All probes use AdamW for 60
epochs with learning rate 0.003, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and batch size 2,048.

\subsection{Model selection and evaluation separation}

All selection uses the 10,000-example validation split.  For the final
samplewise comparison, the selection set contains exactly the residual-state
method and its matched $M=0$ reference.  Their configurations and three
validation runs are fixed in a machine-readable ledger.  The residual-state
configuration is then frozen and evaluated in five predetermined final runs.
The Local SupCon grid, objective interventions, direct-covariance
control, and other mechanism analyses remain validation-only.  No final result
selects among alternative \TeReLS{} configurations.

\subsection{Evaluation and uncertainty}

We report raw accuracies, means, sample standard deviations, and small-sample
Student-$t$ intervals.  Contrasts are paired only when methods share the same
runs; comparisons across independently seeded evaluation sets are descriptive.
Effective rank, feature variance, off-diagonal correlation, nearest-centroid
accuracy, and between/within-class scatter diagnose representation geometry.
The supplement contains the frozen protocol, selection ledger, and raw output
for every reported run.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Samplewise \TeReLS{} learns a useful representation}

The frozen \TeReLS{} configuration attains
$\FinalTeReLSAccuracy\pm\FinalTeReLSSD$\% on held-out MNIST.  It processes one
observation per optimizer step, sees the fitting split twice, and retains no
temporal graph.  All final runs update both feedforward and residual-lateral
matrices and remain noncollapsed.

The validation comparison changes only residual inhibition.  Accuracy is
$95.51\pm0.02$\% with inhibition and $92.51\pm0.11$\% for the $M=0$ reference.  The
paired gain is $3.01$ points, with 95\% Student-$t$ interval $[2.71,3.31]$.
Effective rank is 83.7 for \TeReLS{} and 80.3 for the reference; the accuracy
gain does not come from a lower-dimensional collapse.

\subsection{The three objective terms have distinct roles}

The validation-only mechanism study changes one factor at a time under the
\TeReLBatched{} protocol.  Removing the temporal term reduces accuracy by 8.18
points without collapsing effective rank.  Shuffling the class-persistent
order reduces accuracy by 7.55 points.  Temporal coherence supplies
the task-aligned teaching signal in this controlled experiment.

The other two terms prevent different degeneracies.  Without gated variance
expansion, the median feature variance falls to $7.7\times10^{-5}$.  Without
decorrelation, feature variance remains high but effective rank falls to 2.8.
BatchNorm alone prevents neither failure.  These interventions assign distinct
empirical functions to the temporal, variance, and decorrelation terms, as
predicted by the soft-SFA formulation.

\subsection{Natural temporal order on PAMAP2}

The PAMAP2 comparison changes only the order presented to the encoder.
Architecture, initialization, optimizer, sample count, and probe remain fixed,
and activity classes are absent from encoder batches.  Chronological order
attains macro-F1 0.307; shuffled order attains 0.312.  Their paired interval,
$[-0.063,0.053]$, includes advantages in either direction.  Every method also
shows a large shift from validation subject 7 to test subject 8.  This single
heterogeneous split resolves neither an ordered nor a shuffled
advantage.  Testing natural-order transfer requires several validation subjects
and tighter control of subject shift.

\subsection{Execution cost}

\TeReLS{} performs 100,000 encoder updates: one for every observation in two
training presentations.  Its detached dynamic state contains 658,434 scalars
for the two-layer encoder, compared with 330,754 for a single-matrix
samplewise reference.  The extra matrix and four settling steps improve the
local rule but add quadratic state and computation.  Encoder fitting takes
$571\pm59$ CPU seconds per final run in the present PyTorch implementation.
These measurements establish bounded temporal storage, not throughput or
energy efficiency.

\section{Limitations}

The main evidence uses a synthetic, label-derived temporal relation.  It shows
that the rule can exploit informative adjacency; it does not show that useful
adjacency can be discovered without labels.  Rapid or aliased latent causes can
make neighboring observations misleading.  PAMAP2 adds one natural-order test,
but its subject shift and single split leave the question unresolved.  Video,
audio, and other continuous streams require direct evaluation across several
independent sequences and timescales.

Local in time does not mean small in width.  Two dense lateral matrices require
$O(D^2)$ state and same-layer communication, and the settling dynamics add
$Q$ matrix--vector products per observation.  Sparse or low-rank operators are
promising only if they preserve the representation gain.  The implementation
also uses an exact activation derivative, AdamW, and synchronized dense linear
algebra.  The locality claim concerns information paths; it is not a claim of
biological realism, low energy, or efficient present-day hardware.

Finally, \TeReLS{} and \TeReLBatched{} differ in more than temporal
detachment.  They use different batch sizes, normalization, schedules, and
numbers of optimizer updates.  Their accuracy gap cannot be assigned to any
one of these factors without a matched optimization study.  The controlled
claim is narrower: residual-state inhibition improves the otherwise identical
samplewise rule, while the batched relaxation shows the performance available
when temporal locality is relaxed.

\section{Conclusion}

The soft-SFA objective defines more than three gradient terms: it defines a
regularized target.  Tracking the residual to that target gives each neuron a
state that factors the feedforward gradient and supplies both endpoints of an
anti-Hebbian lateral update.  \TeReLS{} uses this construction without an error
path across layers or time.

Under controlled MNIST adjacency, residual-state inhibition improves a matched
samplewise reference by 3.01 points and the frozen method reaches
$\FinalTeReLSAccuracy\pm\FinalTeReLSSD$\% in final evaluation.  The batched
relaxation reaches 97.30\%, while mechanism controls distinguish temporal
teaching, variance support, and decorrelation.  These results establish deep
slow-feature learning local in space and time for the controlled relation.  A
general result for natural temporal order remains open.

\section{Gradient derivations}\label{app:gradients}

For detached $p,m,v,A$, the three activation derivatives are
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial L_S}{\partial z_t}
&=\frac{2I_t}{N_ID}(z_t-p_t),\\
\frac{\partial L_V}{\partial z_t}
&=-\frac{2}{BD}g\odot(z_t-m),\\
\frac{\partial L_C}{\partial z_t}
&=\frac{1}{BD}A(z_t-m).
\end{align}
Factoring out $2\lambda_S/(BD)$ leaves exactly $r_t$ in
\cref{eq:activation-residual}.  Because $\hat z_t$ is detached,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial z_t}
\frac{\lambda_S}{BD}\|z_t-\hat z_t\|^2
=\frac{2\lambda_S}{BD}r_t.
\end{equation}
Mapping through the activation gives $b_t=J_{\phi,t}^\top r_t$.  At zero
residual-state coupling, $s_t=b_t$ and the preactivation target obeys
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial W}
\frac{\lambda_S}{BD}\sum_t\|a_t-\stopgrad(a_t-b_t)\|^2
=\frac{2\lambda_S}{BD}\sum_t b_tx_t^\top,
\end{equation}
which equals the chain rule for \cref{eq:terel}.  Nonzero $\kappa$ replaces
$b_t$ by the inhibited state $s_t$ and deliberately modifies this direction.
With an undetached temporal reference, $z_t$ also appears as the predecessor in
pair $t+1$, yielding \cref{eq:batch-gradient}.

\TeReLBatched{} uses $h_{\mathcal B}=N_{\eta,\mathcal B}(a_{\mathcal B})$ and
$z_t=\phi(h_t)$, where $N$ is training-mode BatchNorm.  Let
$e_s=\partial L/\partial z_s$, $r_s=e_s\odot\phi'(h_s)$, and
$J^N_{st}=\partial h_s/\partial a_t$.  Its exact same-layer chain rule is
\begin{equation}
u_t=\sum_{s\in\mathcal B}(J^N_{st})^\top r_s,\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}=\sum_tu_tx_t^\top,\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial c}=\sum_tu_t,
\end{equation}
with
$\partial L/\partial\eta=\sum_s(\partial h_s/\partial\eta)^\top r_s$ for the
affine parameters.  BatchNorm mixes examples within each coordinate; this
expression admits no error from another learned layer.

For the direct covariance control, let $Z_c$ stack centered rows and
$C=\offdiag(Z_c^\top Z_c/B)$.  Since $C$ is symmetric,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial Z_c}\frac{\|C\|_F^2}{D}
=\frac{4}{BD}Z_cC,
\end{equation}
which is $4C(z_t-m)/(BD)$ in column-vector notation.  \TeReL{} replaces
$C$ by the stored $A$ and absorbs the positive factor into $\lambda_C$.

\section{Implementation-fidelity tests}\label{app:tests}

The public test suite verifies the following properties:
\begin{itemize}
  \item equality between the soft-objective gradient and the detached target
  gradient at zero residual-state coupling, including the variance sign and
  temporal boundary;
  \item the presynaptic--neuron-state outer product, inhibitory equilibrium and
  finite-step dynamics, and use of the same detached states in the lateral
  moment;
  \item nonzero updates to every encoder layer and both lateral states, absence
  of cross-layer gradients, fixed dynamic-state size, and release of every
  samplewise graph;
  \item training-split-only BatchNorm calibration that changes running buffers
  while preserving every random-encoder weight, bias, and affine parameter;
  \item the MNIST split roles, PAMAP2 subject roles, normalization fit only on
  the training split, temporal boundaries, and encoder batches containing only features and boundary
  batches;
  \item matched all-layer and final-layer probes, greedy layerwise scheduling,
  execution of every declared backpropagation layer, and exact counting of data
  presentations;
  \item batch-SFA constraints, boundary-aware derivatives, and numerical
  agreement of the published-algorithm IncSFA port with its reference; and
  \item refusal to execute final evaluation after any change to the frozen
  protocol, selection ledger, configuration, or tracked source.
\end{itemize}
